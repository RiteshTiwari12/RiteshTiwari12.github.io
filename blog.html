Euler’s Identity

December 2025

Among all equations in mathematics, Euler’s identity is often regarded as the most elegant. It establishes a deep connection between seemingly unrelated mathematical constants in a single, compact expression:

$$ e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0 $$

This identity involves five fundamental constants: the base of the natural logarithm $e$, the imaginary unit $i$, the geometric constant $\pi$, and the additive and multiplicative identities $0$ and $1$. Each of these constants originates from a different branch of mathematics, making their appearance together especially remarkable.

Euler’s Formula

The identity follows directly from Euler’s formula, which states that for any real number $x$,

$$ e^{ix} = \cos x + i \sin x. $$

Euler’s formula itself can be derived using the Taylor series expansions of the exponential, sine, and cosine functions. It provides a powerful link between exponential growth and trigonometric behavior.

Derivation of the Identity

Substituting $x = \pi$ into Euler’s formula gives

$$ e^{i\pi} = \cos \pi + i \sin \pi. $$

Since $\cos \pi = -1$ and $\sin \pi = 0$, this simplifies to

$$ e^{i\pi} = -1. $$

Adding $1$ to both sides yields Euler’s identity.

Geometric Interpretation

On the complex plane, the expression $e^{ix}$ represents a point on the unit circle. As $x$ varies, the point rotates counterclockwise around the circle. When $x = \pi$, the point lies on the negative real axis at $-1$. This geometric viewpoint provides an intuitive explanation of Euler’s identity.

Why Is It Considered Beautiful?

Euler’s identity is often praised for its simplicity and depth. It unifies algebra, geometry, analysis, and complex numbers in a single statement. Few equations manage to capture such a wide range of mathematical ideas so concisely.

For many mathematicians, Euler’s identity serves as an example of the inherent harmony within mathematics — a reminder that deep connections often exist beneath the surface of seemingly separate concepts.

Understanding Epsilon–Delta Notation

December 2025

One of the most fundamental — and often challenging — concepts in real analysis is the rigorous definition of a limit using epsilon–delta notation. While intuitive ideas of limits are common from calculus, epsilon–delta definitions provide the precise logical foundation.

The Intuitive Idea of a Limit

Informally, saying that a function $f(x)$ approaches a value $L$ as $x$ approaches $a$ means that the values of $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $a$. Epsilon–delta notation formalizes this idea.

The Formal Definition

We say that

$$ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L $$

if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that whenever $0 < |x - a| < \delta$, it follows that

$$ |f(x) - L| < \varepsilon. $$

In this definition, $\varepsilon$ represents how close we want the function values to be to the limit, while $\delta$ determines how close $x$ must be to the point $a$.

Understanding the Roles of $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$

The key idea is that $\varepsilon$ is chosen first, independently of $\delta$. Once an acceptable error tolerance $\varepsilon$ is fixed, we must then find a corresponding $\delta$ that guarantees the inequality $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$.

This order of quantifiers is essential: the function must work for every $\varepsilon > 0$, no matter how small.

An Example

Consider the function $f(x) = 2x$. We claim that

$$ \lim_{x \to 1} 2x = 2. $$

Given any $\varepsilon > 0$, we want $|2x - 2| < \varepsilon$. This inequality simplifies to

$$ |x - 1| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}. $$

Therefore, choosing $\delta = \varepsilon / 2$ ensures the required condition. This verifies the limit using epsilon–delta notation.

Why Epsilon–Delta Matters

Although epsilon–delta arguments can initially seem abstract, they establish the logical rigor underlying calculus. Many advanced concepts in analysis — such as continuity, differentiability, and uniform convergence — rely on similar definitions.

Mastering epsilon–delta notation is an important step toward thinking rigorously and precisely in mathematics.
